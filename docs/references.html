References and Further Reading

Cited in this course

The learning-design advice in this course rests on a small number of well-known sources: planning from the outcome backwards (Wiggins and McTighe 2005), writing objectives with concrete verbs (Anderson and Krathwohl 2001), limiting how much is new at once (Sweller 1988), combining words with pictures (Mayer 2009), and using questions to help learners remember (Roediger and Karpicke 2006). The accessibility habits follow the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (World Wide Web Consortium 2023).

Anderson, Lorin W., and David R. Krathwohl, eds. 2001. A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing: A Revision of Bloom’s Taxonomy of Educational Objectives. New York: Longman.
Mayer, Richard E. 2009. Multimedia Learning. 2nd ed. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.
Roediger, Henry L., and Jeffrey D. Karpicke. 2006. “Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention.” Psychological Science 17 (3): 249–55.
Sweller, John. 1988. “Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning.” Cognitive Science 12 (2): 257–85.
Wiggins, Grant, and Jay McTighe. 2005. Understanding by Design. 2nd ed. Alexandria, VA: ASCD.
World Wide Web Consortium. 2023. “Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2.” W3C Recommendation. https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/.

Documentation of the tools

  • Quarto, the program that builds the website: quarto.org/docs/guide. The section on Websites and the one on Visual Editor are the most useful.

Suggested next steps

  • Read one of the sources above in full. The book on Understanding by Design changes how many people plan every course afterwards.
  • Take one chapter of an existing lecture of yours and turn it into a chapter of a course, using the skeleton in _chapter-skeleton.qmd.
  • Ask a colleague to review your first chapter using the checklist in Chapter 7.
